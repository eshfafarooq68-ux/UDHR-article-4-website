Article 4 — Freedom from Slavery

Universal Declaration of Human Rights · Article 4

Freedom from slavery

Select a phrase to see what it means

Three words, three meanings

Slavery, servitude and the slave trade

Article 4 uses each term on purpose. They overlap, but each one was defined by a separate body of international law.

Slavery

Ownership over a person

The 1926 Slavery Convention defines it as the status of a person over whom any or all of the powers attached to the right of ownership are exercised. The test is control, not paperwork.

Servitude

Unfree, without ownership

The 1956 Supplementary Convention covers debt bondage, serfdom, forced marriage, and handing over a child to be exploited. The person is not legally owned, but cannot leave.

Slave trade

Moving people for sale

Capturing, acquiring or disposing of a person to enslave them, and every act of buying, selling or transporting an enslaved person. Modern law treats human trafficking as its closest relative.

How the ban was built

A prohibition that took two centuries

The Declaration of 1948 did not start the movement. It put an existing rule into the first global statement of human rights.

  1. 1807
    The United Kingdom outlaws the slave trade

    The Slave Trade Act bans trading in enslaved people across the British Empire.

  2. 1833
    The Slavery Abolition Act

    Passed by the UK Parliament and in force from 1834, it ends slavery across most of the empire, with payment to former owners rather than to the people freed.

  3. 1865
    The US Thirteenth Amendment

    It abolishes slavery in the United States, with an exception for punishment of a crime.

  4. 1888
    Brazil ends slavery

    It is the last country in the Americas to do so.

  5. 1926
    The Slavery Convention

    The League of Nations adopts the first international definition of slavery.

  6. 1930
    ILO Forced Labour Convention (No. 29)

    Requires states to suppress forced or compulsory labour.

  7. 1948
    Article 4 of the Universal Declaration

    The UN General Assembly adopts the Declaration in Paris on 10 December, with no country voting against it.

  8. 1956
    The Supplementary Convention

    Extends the ban to debt bondage, serfdom and similar practices.

  9. 1966
    The Covenant on Civil and Political Rights

    Article 8 turns the ban into a treaty duty. It entered into force in 1976 and states cannot suspend it, even in an emergency.

  10. 2000
    The Palermo Protocol

    The first international definition of human trafficking, adopted as part of the UN convention against organized crime.

Where things stand

The ban is universal. The practice is not gone.

Recognising it

What the ban means in practice

Signs of forced labour

From the ILO's list of indicators

  • Withheld wages. Pay is delayed, cut or never given.
  • Held documents. An employer keeps a passport or identity card.
  • Debt bondage. A debt for recruitment, transport or housing that never shrinks.
  • Restricted movement. The worker is locked in, watched, or cut off from family.
  • Threats and violence. Including threats of reporting someone to the authorities.
  • Deception. The job, pay or conditions differ from what was promised.

What states are asked to do

Duties that follow from Article 4

  • Criminalise it. Make slavery, servitude, trafficking and forced labour punishable offences.
  • Investigate and prosecute. Enforce the law against traffickers and abusive employers.
  • Protect people. Identify victims, shelter them, and avoid punishing them for acts they were forced to commit.
  • Prevent it. Regulate recruiters, inspect workplaces and address the poverty that makes people vulnerable.
